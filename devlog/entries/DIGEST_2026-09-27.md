# 2026-09-27 — Act One becomes four real places to walk

> Where the game used to start you in a fishing village with no memory of
> how you got there, a new run now opens on a windswept coast, at a
> windmill above a breakwater — the first stop on a four-map road through
> a forest, a mountain range, and a candlelit underworld, each one drawn
> from its own hand-made engraving. A sealed vault door at the bottom of
> that road now actually leads somewhere. Underneath all of it, a
> five-part clean-out of the game's engine turned up a hard truth: raising
> your character's stats has never done anything in a fight. And for now,
> that finding pauses new cards — the designer wants to purge the deck
> down to two starter cards and rebuild it with stats that actually matter,
> in a session with him, not the loop working alone.

## [mechanics] Act One becomes four real places to walk

**What:** A new game now starts on the coast, at a windmill overlooking a
stretch of shoreline called **The Breakwater** — not in the fishing
village that used to open every run. From there, the road runs coast →
**The Charcoal Wood** → **The Beacon Crags** → **The Lantern Deep**, four
brand-new maps, each built from its own square engraving (coast, forest,
mountain fastness, candlelit underworld) instead of the placeholder art
the old maps used. Each map now has its own canvas sized to its art, so
the map can spread up, down, left, and right from wherever you enter it,
rather than only climbing in one direction. Every landmark drawn on each
engraving got its own node to stand on. Each region carries one elite
fight — the toughest battle before its exit — and the last one, the
Lantern Deep, ends at a sealed vault door that finally leads into the
Aporia, the Labyrinth's underworld, instead of only being reachable
through a developer menu. The old seven maps (fishing village, northern
forest, the caverns, and onward) aren't gone — they're still fully
playable, just waiting their turn to be folded into a later chapter of
the story. Combat's background art is intentionally plain black for now
on every map, old and new alike, until Act One gets its own painted
arenas.

**Why:** The maps a new player actually walks were placeholders — grids
you climbed one column at a time, textureless, disconnected from the
world's atmosphere. The game's designer generated four proper engravings,
one per Act One region, and ruled (in a series of decisions recorded
2026-09-25/26) that the new run should open on them immediately rather
than waiting for every later chapter to be ready first. The road order
(coast, forest, mountains, underworld last) and the Labyrinth door's
placement — right on the vault-door landmark drawn into the underworld
plate — are both the designer's direct calls, not loop inference. The
elite-per-region shape gives each map a real capstone fight instead of
ending on a soft whimper.

## [infra] A five-part engine clean-out finds the character stats do nothing in a fight

**What:** A five-part audit and cleanup pass (nicknamed "Trim the Fat"
internally) went through the combat engine, the mobile app, the docs, and
the loop's own planning notes, and deleted a large pile of dead code: a
legacy dice-rolling pipeline nobody used anymore, a shelved visual-effects
system, eight unused tuning commands, and stale documentation describing
systems that no longer exist. Along the way, the audit found something
more consequential than any of the cleanup itself: the combat engine never
actually reads a character's body, mind, or heart stat when calculating
card damage. The only thing those stats currently affect is your maximum
health. Raising a stat you chose to specialize in makes no combat
difference at all.

**Why:** That finding changes what comes next more than any of the
cleanup does. Building a real connection between a character's stats and
their card damage requires deciding, with the designer, exactly which
stat should drive which kind of damage — not a call the loop can make on
its own. Rather than keep shipping small card and keyword changes into a
deck that's about to be rebuilt around that new stat hook, the designer
ruled the loop should pause card and keyword creation entirely: once the
stat hook ships, every player card gets purged except the two starting
cards (a plain blow and a plain block), and the deck gets rebuilt from
there with him in the room. Until then, no steward or loop verb creates a
new player card or keyword.

## [ui] The character sheet's GRACE header stopped hiding under another panel

**What:** On the character screen, the GRACE section's heading was
sliding up underneath the panel above it (labeled THE ACCOUNT), so the
heading was partly obscured instead of sitting cleanly above its own
section.

**Why:** An earlier change removed a section that used to sit directly
above GRACE, but a leftover negative top-margin on the GRACE heading —
tuned to tuck neatly under that now-deleted section — stayed in place and
pulled the heading up into whatever now sat above it instead. Dropping the
stale margin lets GRACE sit where its own spacing says it should.

## [content] The trim's cleanup ripples into cards, loot, and keyword text

**What:** Three cards (*Miserere*, *Dead Pledge*, *The Note Falls Due*)
had a die-roll bonus tied to a mechanic ("fate") that an earlier change
already deleted, so the line printed on the card face never actually did
anything — it's now a real, working bonus instead of dead text. A fourth
card, *The Unpaid Sexton*, said "drafted die" on its face when the game
no longer drafts dice, so its copy now says "rolled dice" instead. Loot
caches also stopped offering roughly half of their consumable-item pool,
after an earlier item-system cleanup made those items impossible to use —
caches now only ever offer consumables that actually do something. The
keyword glossary got a matching pass: several entries still described
mechanics ("fate", a save bonus, a resist roll) that no longer exist in
the shipped game.

**Why:** All four threads trace back to the same cause — the trim
cleanup deleted mechanics that some cards, item pools, and keyword text
still assumed were live, leaving card faces, loot tables, and the
glossary telling players about rules that no longer apply. A card whose
printed text does nothing, or a loot pool that hands out dead weight half
the time, erodes trust in what the game tells you before you commit to a
play.
